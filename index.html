Same average return, reversed order, $41,769 apart

Two retirees hold the same $600,000 and withdraw $30,000 a year at the end of each year, after that year's return. The market hands both the same five annual returns: one bad year at minus 20 percent and four at plus 10. One retiree takes the crash in year one, the other in year five. The five returns average +4 percent a year and compound to the same 17.1 percent total either way, yet the first ends year five at $519,615 and the second at $561,384. Order alone moved the outcome by $41,769, about 1.4 years of withdrawals.

The damage has a mechanical source: shares sold at the bottom. The crash-year withdrawal for the year-one retiree comes out of a $480,000 balance, 6.25 percent of the portfolio sold at depressed prices. The year-five retiree's crash-year withdrawal comes out of $591,384, 5.07 percent. The extra 1.18 points of the portfolio sold at the low price never participate in the recovery, which is why the identical compound return cannot reconcile the two ledgers. An average return is order-blind; a withdrawal schedule is not.

The fix is a spending rule with a hinge. Cut the crash-year withdrawal from $30,000 to $24,000, 4 percent of the starting balance instead of 5, and leave the other four years at $30,000: the year-one retiree ends year five at $528,400 instead of $519,615, $8,785 ahead for one flexible year. That recovers about a fifth of the gap luck created. Measured as runway, the rigid plan ends year five holding 17.3 years of withdrawals and the lucky-order plan 18.7, and a single trim is worth more in a crash year than in any calm one.
